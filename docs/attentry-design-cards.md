# Attentry Design: direction cards (second draft, 2026-11-23)

One card per element. Each says what the element **causes** in the scene and how to show it.
Cards are picked by the elements a request or storyboard names, plus the **pairs** at the end
(what happens between two elements). First draft 2026-11-19 from Block, Thomas & Johnston,
Williams, Blazer, Shaw and Albers; second draft from the direction books (Yot, Gurney, Gilland,
Bousquet, Mateu-Mestre, Glebas; notes and page references in attentry-design-book-notes.md).
Sources in brackets: [Yot 36] = Yot, p. 36. Every card from the first draft is still here,
corrected where the books disagreed (water as a mirror, falloff in sunlight, grey shadows, night).

**One rule over all cards** [Gurney 210; Yot 18]: decide where the light comes from (sun, sky,
fire, lamps) and keep it the same for every element in the shot. Derive fill from the scene's own
sources and their bounce, not from a studio three-point setup.

## Light

### Sun, clear day
- **Three lights, not one** [Yot 12; Gurney 28]: the sun (small, hard, white: sharp shadows), the
  blue sky (huge, soft: lights the shadows blue) and light bounced off the ground (warm, from
  below). The sky and bounce stay weaker than the sun.
- Shadows hold color, never neutral grey or black [Yot 64]: blue under open sky, green under
  leaves, greyer as clouds increase [Gurney 28].
- Outdoors, cast shadows are darker than the shaded sides of objects (those get bounce from the
  ground) [Yot 55]. The terminator, where light turns to shadow, is the darkest band [Yot 55].
- No falloff: the sun is too far away for light to dim across a scene [Yot 76].
- Every surface crossing into the same shadow drops by the same amount [Gurney 52].

### Sun by time of day [Yot 26–37; Gurney 180–183]
- **Midday:** white, hard, short near-black shadows, washed-out color. Hard to make pretty: say why.
- **Golden hour:** warm low light, long shadows, texture raked out, sky above a richer blue.
- **Sunset:** weak orange or red sun, low contrast, long shadows, richer blue or purple shadows,
  clouds lit from below; the strongest glow where the sun meets the horizon, a weaker one opposite.
  It lasts minutes: don't hold it static for a long scene.
- **Dusk:** no sun; soft light, almost no shadow, delicate color, a pink band (alpenglow) in the
  sky opposite the sunset; after sunset the earth's grey shadow rises on that side.
- **Overcast:** one huge soft light from above; shadows nearly gone except under cars and trees;
  colors purer, sky the lightest thing [Gurney 30; Yot 33].

### Moon / night
- **The sky stays lighter than any unlit land** [Yot 36]. Land brighter than the sky needs a light
  source on it.
- Moonlight follows sunlight's rules, much fainter; the moon is small (resist making it huge), red
  or yellow near the horizon, white higher up [Yot 36].
- Night reads as shapes disappearing, not as everything painted dark: keep the sky's value, lose
  the detail on the land [Mateu-Mestre 054]. It looks blue to the eye though it measures neutral
  [Gurney 138]; show what the eye sees, not the camera's black shadows [Gurney 39].
- One warm light against the blue gives night its contrast [Block]. Stars: small, a few twinkle.

### Fire
- **It's the scene's light source.** Light falls off fast with distance: bright within one or two
  object-lengths, a warm glow beyond, dark at the edges [Yot 42, 50].
- So red the eye can't correct it [Yot 50]. It sits low (ground, table), so it lights faces and
  objects from below: the usual light and shade reverse. That reads as cozy only with other light
  around; alone it reads as menace [Yot 24; Gurney 64].
- Objects lit only on the side facing the fire; long shadows away from it, moving with the flicker.
- Flicker is irregular: small wobbles several times a second, an occasional flare; the light on the
  ground flickers with the flames.
- How it moves [Gilland 166–192, 237]: a fluid, like a flag waving upward in rising hot air; volume
  changes all the time; tips break off and vanish in two or three frames; no two flames alike, no
  symmetry.
- Color by heat [Bousquet 250]: hottest white or blue at the base and tip, yellow-orange-red
  inside, red where cooler. Gas burns blue and clean; wood smokes.
- Gives off smoke and embers (see those cards). A bright fire at night can seem smokeless, because
  the eye adjusts to the flame [Bousquet 257].

### Lamp, screen, neon, street light
- Same falloff as fire, steadier. A colored cast on nearby surfaces [Yot 42].
- Indoor bulbs look whiter than they measure: render them warm-white, not orange [Yot 44].
- Street lights: small pools, fast falloff, no fill, very high contrast; between two lights, two
  shadows pointing different ways [Yot 51]. Sodium is orange, older mercury lights blue-green
  [Gurney 39].
- Neon and signs are among the few truly saturated colors, because they emit light [Yot 108].
- A very bright source has a colored halo that lifts the values next to it [Gurney 166].

### Window light
- The window is the source: soft, with visible falloff into the room [Yot 42].
- Bluish from the sky, floor tinted by the sky, ceiling tinted green or orange by the ground outside
  [Gurney 32]. Against warm lamps inside, cool versus warm.

## Shadows

- Every lit object casts one, away from the light, on whatever is below or behind it.
- **Sharp only where the object touches**, softer the farther it is from the surface [Bousquet
  127–128]; a large or near light softens all edges, a small or far one sharpens them [Yot 58].
- **Contact and occlusion shadows:** a thin dark line where an object meets the ground and where
  surfaces come close, even on an overcast day with no cast shadows; in soft light often the
  darkest thing in the picture [Yot 56; Gurney 54; Bousquet 133].
- Something flying low casts a shadow that grows sharper and closer as it descends.
- Shadow color is the sum of what lights it: in shadow, up-facing planes are cool (sky),
  down-facing warm (ground bounce) [Gurney 67].
- Several lights make several shadows [Yot 60].
- Large pure-black shadows read as holes in the picture; pure-white highlights look pasted on
  [Glebas 210].
- With a low camera, where feet and objects touch the ground must match their spacing, or things
  read as giant [Mateu-Mestre 071].

## Air and particles

### Smoke
- From one point: a straight column, then roughly parallel strands, then swirls, then chaos where
  it spreads and stops rising; with wind, the same at an angle [Bousquet 256].
- Damp wood makes puffy smoke, dry wood thin tendrils; above flames the smoke continues the flames'
  motion [Bousquet 256–257].
- A little is enough: smoke that hides the subject is a mistake.

### Embers / sparks
- A few, not a shower. They ride the same air currents as the smoke, erratically, and fade before
  the top of the frame [Gilland 225].

### Wind
- Moves everything light the same way: smoke, flames, grass, hair, cloth, clouds (one direction for
  the whole scene). Soft parts trail what they're attached to and settle after it stops [Bousquet
  218].
- Clouds at different heights move at different speeds; their wispy edges change faster than the
  mass [Bousquet 229].

### Haze, fog and depth
- Distance makes things fainter, bluer, lower in contrast; the darks change first [Yot 38; Gurney
  176]. Haze takes the sky's color (blue in sun, white overcast, orange at sunset) [Yot 130].
- It happens only where the air in between is lit [Gurney 177]. Looking toward a low sun through
  mist or dust, the distance turns warmer instead [Gurney 178].
- Thick fog: light from every direction, no shadows under trees, contrast gone within a short
  distance, distant color drops out [Yot 38; Gurney 184].

### Sun rays (god rays)
- Need all three [Gurney 190]: a screen (clouds, leaves, a roof) with a few openings, dark behind;
  dust, mist or smoke in the air; and a view toward the sun. Facing away from the sun, no rays.
- The rays are parallel but converge on the sun's position in perspective [Bousquet 230].

### Dappled light
- Gaps in the leaves project images of the sun: round spots, ellipses on slanted surfaces, larger
  and softer under a higher canopy; they move when the treetops move [Gurney 192–193].

### Cloud shadows
- Soft edges (a long transition from sun to shade); their size and spacing must match the clouds in
  the sky; darker and cooler, less blue than a clear-day shadow [Gurney 194].
- A patch of sun is a spotlight: put it on the subject.

### Wings / anything that flaps
- **Each down-beat pushes air down.** On water: a ring of ripples under it on every down-beat. On
  dust, snow, sand, grass: a puff or a bend. In cloud: wisps pushed aside.
- The body rises a little on the down-beat and sinks on the up-beat.
- The strongest beat (the climax) gets the biggest effect: spray, a dust cloud.

## Water

### Calm water
- **Seen from a low angle it mirrors what's above** (sky, sun, anything near it), upside down;
  **seen from above you look into it** and the mirror fades [Bousquet 286; Gurney 200].
- Reflections of bright things are darker than the things [Gurney 199]. Ripples break up
  horizontals and keep verticals: reflections stretch into vertical streaks [Gurney 201].
- Deep water is blue in sun, blue-grey overcast, blue-black at night; shallow water is clear and
  shows the bottom, warmer and darker underwater [Bousquet 289; Gurney 202].
- A low sun lays a glitter path toward the viewer, flickering.
- Displacement: anything moving in it disturbs it; it can't stay still [Gilland 35–37].

### Ripples
- Rings that spread and fade over about a second, flattened into ellipses by perspective.
- Real ripples overlap and interfere unevenly; perfect concentric circles look fake [Gilland 129].
- Every disturbance makes them: a wing beat, a fish, a drop, a footstep at the edge.

### Splash [Gilland 70–110]
- **Two events:** the primary splash (a crown or sheet thrown out), then, after a beat of about
  half a second, the secondary jet up the middle as the hole the object punched fills in.
- Which one dominates depends on the object:

  | Object | Primary | Secondary jet |
  |---|---|---|
  | Small fast stone, raindrop | almost none | clear |
  | Flat rock, belly-flop | big | medium |
  | Cannonball, heavy log | big | bigger |
  | Floating ball | only this | none (no air pocket) |
  | Clean dive | tiny | tiny |

- The sheet tears into holes along the direction it was thrown, within about a second becomes
  strings of droplets, then falls under gravity. Spray follows arcs and falls back [Bousquet 289].
- **Size is timing:** a big splash moves slower; the same splash at small-splash speed reads as a
  miniature [Gilland 121].

### Rain, snow, fog
- Rain: long blurred streaks, grey-white in daylight, angled by the wind; ripples or splashes where
  they land; wet surfaces reflect [Bousquet 239].
- Snow: slow, drifting, settles on top edges; whiter than clouds; shadows on snow take the sky's
  color; small shadows on it are soft [Gurney 198].
- Fog: see Haze, fog and depth.

### Underwater
- Red disappears within about ten feet, orange and yellow by twenty-five; then blue [Gurney 204].
- Moving caustic patterns only near the surface, on sunny days, on up-facing surfaces [Gurney 161;
  Yot 98].

## Motion and force

### Falling and thrown things
- Gravity speeds things up evenly: spacing between frames grows 1, 3, 5, 7 falling and shrinks
  7, 5, 3, 1 rising [Bousquet 50]. Size and weight don't change it; only air resistance slows wide
  light things [Bousquet 63].
- Broken pieces fly roughly equal and opposite, mostly sideways, then fall in arcs [Bousquet 265].

### Explosions [Gilland 248–275; Bousquet 255–261]
- Realistically over in about two frames; play it over 24–30 or it can't be seen.
- Shape in time: an instant of pure outward energy (the first frames look alike whatever the
  material), a violent burst over a few frames, a sharp slowing, a flash, then a long slow expansion
  and fade (seconds). A small blast's fireball burns out within a second; its cloud lingers 3–6 s.
- Debris peaks and falls while the cloud fades; a few pieces always fly much farther than the rest.
- Smoke starts directional, then billows as the air takes over; after the blast front, air rushes
  back and pulls debris inward.

### Scale
- Big things move slower; a giant with a small creature's timing looks like a toy [Gilland 121;
  Bousquet 164].
- Show the size of something huge with a known element in both shots, touching it [Mateu-Mestre
  056].

## Vehicles and speed

### Aircraft taking off
- **The order sells it:** the roll speeds up; the nose lifts first while the main wheels stay down;
  then the main wheels leave the ground; then the landing gear folds up into the body.
- While rolling, a tiny shake from the runway; it stops the moment the wheels leave the ground.
- After lift-off the climb is steady, nose up, not a sudden jump.

### Jet engines
- Heat shimmer behind the exhaust: the background behind it wobbles. You see the air, not a flame.
- At full power on the ground: dust and grass blown back behind it.

### Vehicle lights (aircraft)
- Landing light: a bright cone ahead and down, lighting the runway in front of the wheels.
- A red beacon blinking about once a second; a white strobe flashing at the wingtip.
- Seen from the left side: the red navigation light on the wingtip. Lit cabin windows at dusk.

### Runways and roads at dusk
- Edge lights in rows; they rush past with speed and stay put when the camera stops.
- Markings streak by while the camera follows the vehicle.

### Speed and the camera
- **Speed shows through the background, not the subject.** When the camera follows something fast,
  the ground rushes by and the far background barely moves (parallax: the nearer, the faster).
- Tracking a fast subject, the background blurs radially from the vanishing point, most at the
  frame edges and nearest the camera [Gurney 169].
- Letting the camera slow and stop at the key moment (lift-off) lets the subject pull away.

## Camera and staging

- **The eye goes to the strongest contrast** [Glebas 180; Yot 144]: reserve the lightest light and
  darkest dark for the subject; lower the contrast elsewhere [Glebas 188].
- Read the shot as two or three masses of light and dark first; the subject should be one clear
  mass, and its silhouette should read filled in black [Mateu-Mestre 034; Glebas 91].
- Lines lead the eye: curves calm, diagonals tense, straight lines firm [Mateu-Mestre 026]. The eye
  follows the vanishing point and where the main character looks [Mateu-Mestre 027].
- Avoid near-touching edges between key shapes [Mateu-Mestre 026; Yot 149].
- Big, medium, small: uneven sizes give depth; off-center beats symmetry [Mateu-Mestre 025–027].
- Camera low: the subject towers. Camera high: it shrinks [Mateu-Mestre 040–041]. Wide lens close:
  depth and menace; long lens far: flat and calm [Mateu-Mestre 028–030].
- Backlight isolates a subject in silhouette; colors lose saturation and edges glow [Yot 148;
  Gurney 62]. Edge light needs a darker background [Gurney 60].
- Characters need room in the direction they look or move [Glebas 210].
- At a cut, the new shot's focal point starts where the last one's ended [Mateu-Mestre 055]; stay
  on one side of the line [Mateu-Mestre 089].
- Save the most intense shot for the climax: flat and calm first, then perspective, then close,
  low and wide [Mateu-Mestre 075–079].

## Color

- One strong hue with weaker ones, inside a chosen gamut; most of the frame calm, a small accent
  where the eye should go [Gurney 80, 117, 123].
- Natural color gradates (skies, falloff, distance); large flat fills look artificial [Gurney 84].
- Saturation is rare in nature except in emitted light (sky, fire, neon) and things meant to be
  noticed [Yot 108]. Greens vary leaf to leaf and tree to tree [Gurney 83].
- The daylight sky is two gradations: brighter near the sun, lighter toward the horizon [Gurney 174].
- Backlit leaves, grass and skin glow and saturate; skin goes red at the light-shadow edge [Yot 122,
  131; Gurney 152].
- A change of palette marks a new section; within a scene, keep it [Gurney 133; Yot 158].
- Value reads form; hue can be pushed far without losing the read [Yot 104–110].

## Style

### Pixel art
- **Everything in the same pixel grid, the same palette, the same stepped frame rate.** Smooth
  effects or smooth text on stepped sprites break the style.
- Small palette (about 6–12 colors per scene); shading by palette steps and dithering, not
  gradients.
- Sprite animation at about 8–12 drawings a second.

### Text in a scene
- Sits in the negative space (sky, dark ground), never over the subject or the light source.
- Light over dark or dark over light, never mid-tone on mid-tone [Glebas 188].
- Matches the scene: in pixel art, a pixel font in the same grid; at night, lit in the light's
  color or a cool white.
- Arrives at the scene's pace: slow, calm scenes reveal text slowly; it holds long enough to read.
- One idea, a few words.

### Rainbow (when asked for)
- Opposite the sun, centered on the point directly away from it; a fainter second bow outside with
  colors reversed; the sky between them darker [Gurney 187].

## Pairs (what happens between elements)

| When the scene has… | It also needs… |
|---|---|
| fire + night | light falloff on ground and objects, lit sides facing the fire, long flickering shadows away from it, warm against cool, the sky still lighter than the unlit land |
| fire + faces | light from below; add another source if the mood should be warm, not menacing |
| wings + water | ripples under every down-beat; spray on the strongest beat |
| anything above water | its reflection from a low angle, darker than it, stretched into vertical streaks by the ripples |
| low sun + water | a glitter path toward the viewer; reflected sky colors |
| low sun + any object | a warm rim on the sun side, a long shadow away from the sun, blue in the shadow |
| object + ground | a contact shadow where they meet, sharp at the contact, softer away from it |
| something flying low + ground or water | its shadow below, sharper when lower |
| fire + wind | smoke and flames leaning the same way as everything else |
| trees + sun | dappled round spots on the ground, moving with the wind; glowing backlit leaves |
| sun rays | a screen with openings, particles in the air, the camera facing the sun |
| clouds + sunny land | soft-edged cloud shadows matching the clouds above |
| distance + air | far things lighter, bluer, lower contrast (warmer only toward a low sun in haze) |
| splash + any object | primary and secondary sized by the object table; bigger means slower |
| explosion + ground | debris arcing out and falling, a few far stragglers, a cloud lingering seconds after the flash |
| text + a dark scene | text in the dark space, lit in the light's color or cool white |
| aircraft + dusk | landing light cone on the runway, blinking beacon and strobe, lit windows, runway edge lights |
| aircraft + runway | its shadow below, shrinking and fading as it climbs; a shake that stops at lift-off |
| jet engine + ground | heat shimmer behind it; dust and grass blown back while it's on the ground |
| fast subject + following camera | the ground rushing and blurring, the far background slow; the camera easing to a stop at the key moment |
| wind + any scene | a windsock or flag pointing with it; takeoff into the wind |
